
# Rituals, rituals, rituals

Think of something you believe students should do, reflection say. You have two options. Write a process: a form, a deadline, a tracker, someone to chase compliance. Or make it a ritual: the last ten minutes of every day, everyone writes down what they learned, no tracker, it is simply what we do here.

The difference is what each one runs on. A process runs on the chaser, and the moment the chaser is busy, it stops. A ritual runs on time and habit: it has a fixed slot, everyone knows it is coming, and nobody has to remember it or enforce it. That is the whole trick, a ritual removes the dependence on memory and chasing that makes good practices die.

Consistency over a period of time leads to progress, and consistency comes from rituals. This applies to everything we care about. Reflection can be a ritual. A daily standup can be a ritual. Semester orientation, semester completion and graduation are rituals at a larger scale, and they mark for students that something began, something was endured, something was earned.

Rituals also help with a harder problem: principles that are easy to agree with and hard to live. Take any belief in this book that you nod at but struggle to apply. Attach a small ritual to it. Once a week, ask yourself: where did I get a chance to apply this, and did I? Agreeing with a principle happens once. Applying it needs a recurring prompt, and the ritual is the prompt.

Our side of the deal: we keep rituals small. A few minutes daily is what keeps a ritual alive. Let it grow into an hour of ceremony and people start avoiding it, and then it needs a chaser, which is the thing it existed to remove.
