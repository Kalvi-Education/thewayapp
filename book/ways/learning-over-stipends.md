
# Learning over stipends

A student in one of our early batches turned down an internship because the stipend was ₹10,000 lower than another offer. The offer he refused was at a product company: real systems, a technical mentor, a strong path to a pre-placement offer. The offer he took had him running reports and sending weekly client updates. A year later, his batchmate from the "worse" offer had the PPO and a title. He was applying to the same company and did not clear the first round.

The mistake was not greed. It was optimising the wrong variable. A stipend is spent by the end of the month. What you learned stays, and it compounds into every job you hold after. Put it as arithmetic: ₹15,000 at a company that makes you three times the engineer beats ₹45,000 at one where you stand still, because the tripled skill reprices every offer you get from then on.

It helps to know what a stipend is. To a company, an intern is a cost before they are a return: more guidance, more check-ins, more overhead. Whatever a company's reasons for the amount, a stipend is not a salary, and it is not a measure of your worth yet. We push employers on the thing that matters instead, that our students get real engineering work and not admin tasks with a paycheque attached.

So the sequence for any early-career decision: Will I learn here? Will the work stretch me past what I can already do? Then, does the money clear the threshold of living and commuting without stress? Past that threshold, money is the wrong thing to optimise. A student joins college to learn. Money earned along the way is a bonus, and when it becomes the goal instead, watch what starts happening: hard assignments avoided because they pay nothing, offers judged by the number at the bottom instead of what the work builds.

When a student brings you two offers, ask them to describe what they will be able to do after six months at each. Let them hear their own answer.
