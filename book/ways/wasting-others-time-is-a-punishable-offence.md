
# Wasting others' time is a punishable offence

A system built on trust will eventually be tested by someone looking for the loophole. We saw it with support tickets. Some students worked out that raising a ticket costs them nothing, so why not give it a shot whenever a score looked unfavourable. No downside for the student. But every one of those tickets sent someone on our team to re-verify work that had already been verified, and that someone was now not doing the work that actually helps students.

That is the offence. Not the ticket itself, the tickets are the system working. The offence is using a channel without caring that a person answers it. Every speculative request converts one student's thirty seconds into someone else's hour, and enough of them convert a fast trust-based system into a slow suspicious one.

The line matters here. A genuine question that turns out to be trivial is fine. A good-faith mistake that costs someone an afternoon is fine, and stays fine. What we act on is the deliberate or systematic version: the speculative ticket fired at every unfavourable score, the meeting called with no agenda for people who did not need to be there, the request sent without thirty seconds of checking whether the answer already exists.

And it is punishable, not just discouraged, because if gaming the system carries no cost, the loophole spreads, and we end up replacing trust with verification everywhere, which slows everyone down to catch a few. What the punishment is, and who establishes that a pattern was deliberate, belongs to the conduct process, not to this page and not to whoever happens to be annoyed. The way is the stance: we notice this, we name it, and it has consequences.

Our side: we keep the honest paths fast. If a student is raising tickets because the legitimate route to fix a score is slow or unclear, the waste is ours first.
