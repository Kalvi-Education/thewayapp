
# Principles over processes

Say the way we run assessments is changing this semester, again, because we learnt something last semester. Do you walk students through the new mechanics in detail?

Think about what each choice teaches. Explain every nut and bolt, and students anchor on the nuts and bolts. When those change again, students experience an unstable institution, and trust erodes with the revision. Explain the principle instead, that we assess to know what you have mastered and to catch gaps early, and the principle survives every revision of the mechanics. Students hold onto the why while mentors and systems guide them through whatever the current how is.

So we communicate principles, not processes. Students get the stable commitments: the growth we promise, what we measure and why. The operational machinery stays with the people running it, whose job includes handling its churn.

The same choice applies to what we write down for ourselves. A team can respond to every problem with a longer checklist, and checklists have their place. But a ten-step checklist answers the situation its author imagined, and next month's situation is usually slightly different. A principle, "any change to a student's schedule reaches them before it reaches the notice board", gives the person facing the new situation something to reason from. Simplicity is a feature: a short principle people can hold in their head gets applied. A long document gets consulted at best, and often not even that.

The test when you write anything down: will this still be true in two years? If yes, it is a principle, state it and keep it stable. If no, it is a process, keep it lightweight and expect to change it.

Look at the last announcement you sent. Was it a why that will hold, or a how that will not?
