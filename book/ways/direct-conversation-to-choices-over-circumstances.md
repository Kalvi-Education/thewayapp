
# Direct conversation to choices over circumstances

A student is caught copying in a placement test. When you sit with him, the story comes out: he is in a bad place, his parents are going through a messy divorce, he saw the test as his way out. The circumstances are real and deserve real support. And none of them made him copy. That was a choice, made inside the circumstances.

This is the distinction we ask every mentor to hold. A circumstance can be real, hard, outside the student's control, and deserving of support. What it cannot do is decide for them. Explaining an outcome only by the circumstance hides the choices that existed before it, during it, and after it, and those choices are where accountability lives. An emergency comes and you must leave right now: rarely is it so sudden that you could not inform someone first. That was a choice, sitting right in front of you.

Students do not naturally think this way. School taught them that a good enough reason cancels the miss. So the conversation is the work. A student stops coming to class because his project is not finished and he cannot face arriving without it. Sit with him and lay out the choices he had: seek help, which may not even have occurred to him as an option. Come to class anyway. Submit an outline and ask for time. Message you the evening before. You are not cornering him. You are often showing him choices he did not know he had, and building the habit of pausing to look at the options before acting on the first idea that arrives.

A warning before you start. This will not visibly work the first few times. Thinking in choices is a skill that takes months, and a threat produces compliance today, which makes the shortcut tempting every single week. That is exactly why this stays a way and not a rule. Nobody can force you to have the patient conversation. You choose it.

Our side: you are not expected to carry these conversations on instinct alone. Practising them with other mentors, and asking your Campus Manager to sit in on a hard one, is part of the job.

A ritual helps it stick: once a week, ask yourself where you turned a circumstance story into a choices conversation, and where you reached for the shortcut.
