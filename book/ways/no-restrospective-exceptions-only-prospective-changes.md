
# No retrospective exceptions, only prospective changes

"Sir, the class is struggling. Can we lower the passing criteria from 95% to 90% for this sprint?"

It sounds like kindness. Now think about the student who gave up her weekend to hit 95%. Lower the bar after the fact and you have told her the extra effort was a waste, and told everyone else that waiting for a bailout is a strategy. The mercy goes to the people who fell short, and the cost lands on the people who complied.

So we run on one principle: changes apply forward, never backward. A government that passes a new traffic law today cannot ticket you for how you drove last week. If we raise an attendance bar from December 1, it binds from December 1. The seniors who graduated under the old bar were not treated unfairly, they were treated by the rule that existed at the time. "But the previous batch got away with it" is an argument about history, and rules do not run on history.

There is a second reason to be stubborn here. The moment one retrospective exception is granted, students learn that a well-crafted story can win an exemption, and effort starts flowing into the story instead of the work. The loudest voice starts beating the best effort, and a system that yields to noise teaches everyone to be noisy. When the rule is X, it is X until it changes, and then it is Y from that second forward. That stability is what lets a student plan their semester on solid ground.

Keeping this promise puts a duty on us: since nobody can comply with a rule they have not heard, changes must be announced clearly, dated, and explained before they bind anyone. If a student first learns a rule exists when it is being enforced on them, we have applied it retrospectively, whatever the paperwork says.

Next time an exception request lands on you, check which direction it points. Forward is a conversation. Backward is an answer you already have.
