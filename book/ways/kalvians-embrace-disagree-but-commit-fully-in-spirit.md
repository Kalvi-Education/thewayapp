
# Kalvians embrace disagree but commit (fully in spirit)

We were deciding a major change to how we track student progress. One mentor argued against it for an hour. Edge cases, flawed logic, the risk of demotivating students who were already striving. It got heated. The decision went ahead anyway.

The next morning, that same mentor was in the lab explaining the new system to his students with more conviction than anyone else on the team. Asked how, he said: "I still have my doubts. But once we decided this is the direction the car is moving, I'm not going to be the wheel that tries to turn left while the other three turn right. If I want this to succeed for my students, I have to give it 100%. Half-heartedness is a self-fulfilling prophecy of failure."

Sit with that last line, because it describes a real mechanism. A decision executed at half effort fails, and the failure then looks like proof the decision was wrong, when it was only proof the execution was half. Notice what the record shows in that case: the person who disagreed never blocked anything, never said no, and the decision still died in their hands.

So both halves are obligations. Before the decision: if you see a flaw, say it then, because that is the only time saying it can change the outcome, and we judge the argument, never the person making it. After the decision: you execute it as yours. That is not weakness. You argued from your seat, others argued from theirs, and someone accountable weighed all of it. Trusting that weighing is what lets a team decide anything at all. When things go wrong later, the useful question is "how do we fix it", not "I told you so". And if real evidence arrives that the decision is harming students, that is not a reopened debate, that is new information, and it goes back through the same channel that made the decision.

The failure mode has a name here: the postman. "I personally think this is a bit much, but the central team wants it." To a student, you are Kalvium. Deliver a letter you refuse to sign and the student learns that the program itself does not believe its own decisions.

Which are you more prone to: staying silent before the decision, or staying unconvinced after it?
