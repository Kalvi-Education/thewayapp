
# Fair vs unfair, equality vs equity

"I slept in class and no one woke me. That is unfair." "Sir, you remind him about his journal every day. You never remind me. That is favoritism."

Both complaints use the word fair. The first is an outcome the student dislikes. The second is support the student misread. Neither is a broken rule, and a broken rule is what unfair should mean.

We teach students to embrace that the world outside can be unfair. That is a separate page, and it is not a licence for us. We will not design unfairness into our own system and shrug at it. So we need a working definition, because students often run on a different one: things that feel good are fair, things that do not are unfair. By that definition a surprise test is unfair and an extended deadline is fair, and no rule we write could ever pass it.

Fair means the same rules for everybody. The attendance bar, the integrity line, the submission deadline: one set, applied to all, published where everyone can read them.

Fair does not mean the same support for everybody. One student this semester needs a daily nudge to stay afloat. Another needs to be left alone with a hard problem. Meeting each student where they are, what we call Kalvium for one, can look like favoritism from the outside. It is not. It is equity: same rules, support proportional to need. When a student calls it out, explain exactly that, and be ready to answer what their support looks like.

One more piece. Some discomfort here is designed: the open-ended brief, the hidden test case, the deadline that forces a tradeoff. Working through these is where the growth happens. Our obligation is to know, and be able to say, which discomfort is planned and which is just something broken. "That is part of the design, and here is why" is a fair answer. So is "you are right, that is broken, we will fix it". Guessing is not.

Next time a student says unfair, work out which they mean: an outcome they dislike, support they misread, or a rule we broke or failed to publish. The third needs our apology and a fix. The first two still need an answer, because a student who hears nothing concludes the complaint was right.
