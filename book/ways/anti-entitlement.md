
# Anti-entitlement

"I have paid the fees and spent two years here. I am entitled to an internship."

Follow the logic through. The fee bought an environment: real projects, mentors, deadlines, peers who push you. It did not buy the outcome. The company doing the hiring never sees the receipt. It sees a GitHub profile, a conversation, a person who can or cannot solve the problem in front of them.

Entitlement is the belief that showing up is enough. A student attends every class and completes every assignment, but avoids peer reviews because feedback stings, and never picks the problem that might defeat them. The volume of work is there and the growth is not. When the top placement does not come, they feel cheated. Nobody cheated them. They counted attendance and submissions as growth, and the two are not the same thing. The program around them was working. The part only they could do, seeking feedback that stings, picking problems that might defeat them, did not happen.

Employees are not exempt, and our version is subtler. Say the policy allows 24 work from home days a year, and you planned yours around the semester break. Then the campus schedule shifts and students are back early, needing you on the ground. "But the policy says I have days left" is true and beside the point. A policy marks a boundary. It does not tell you what the mission needs this week. If the students need you there, that is where you are.

The ordering we hold ourselves to: first the student, then the campus, then me. Not because your needs do not matter, but because everyone at Kalvium serves someone whose growth depends on them showing up beyond the minimum.

Notice the question you ask under pressure. Is it "what am I owed here" or "what does this situation need from me"? The first one always has a defensible answer. The second one is the job.
