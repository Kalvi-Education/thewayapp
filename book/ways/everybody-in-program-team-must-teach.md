
# Everybody in Program team must teach

You can design a course from a desk. You can review its completion data, read mentor feedback, and adjust. Do this long enough without facing a classroom and the students you are designing for become the students you remember, while the real ones change every semester.

Reports narrow this gap only partly, because a report carries what someone chose to measure. Standing in front of a class carries the rest: the module that reads well on paper and dies in the second hour, the question eleven students ask that the design assumed nobody would, the exercise that was meant to take twenty minutes and takes sixty.

So if you are in the Program team, you teach. Not as a gesture, and not once a year as a visit. Often enough that your picture of a student is current. In a design debate, the person who taught last month is arguing from what students did. The person who taught two years ago is arguing from what students used to do.

What you learn in the room has to travel back into the design, otherwise the teaching was only a field trip. The module that died in the second hour gets redesigned. The question eleven students asked becomes part of the material. That loop closing is the actual point of the obligation.

There is a second effect worth naming. When students and mentors see the people who design the program delivering it, the design earns credibility. It stops being instructions from somewhere and becomes something its authors visibly stand behind.

When did you last face a room of students with something you built, and what did the room tell you that the data had not?
