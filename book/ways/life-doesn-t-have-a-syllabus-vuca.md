
# Life doesn't have a syllabus (VUCA)

"This is unfair, we weren't taught this framework in the module." "Why does this coding challenge have hidden test cases?" "The company didn't tell us the interview topics beforehand."

When a student says these things to you, they are not being difficult. They are telling you what twelve years of school taught them: everything that can be tested will be announced first. If it's in the textbook it matters, and if it's not, it doesn't exist. That is not a study habit. It is a worldview, and in a professional life it is a dangerous one.

The tempting response is to give them what they ask for. Publish the topic list. Show the test cases. Narrow the project brief. Each of these makes the complaint go away today, and each one trains the student a little deeper into expecting a syllabus from a world that does not issue one. The professional world is volatile, uncertain, complex and ambiguous, VUCA for short. Cassettes went to MP3s went to streaming. Programming went from C to Java to Python to AI-assisted development. COVID arrived with no handbook for any country or company.

So we keep the ambiguity in, on purpose. The hidden test cases in our challenges are the users who will run the student's software on devices nobody tested, because that is what production is. A mock interview without a topic list mirrors how a good company hires, for understanding, not memorisation.

Our side of this: the ambiguity must be designed, not accidental. An open-ended brief works when there is a learning goal behind it and someone ready to give feedback when the student flounders. A brief that is vague because nobody prepared teaches nothing, and students work out the difference within a week.

When a student demands the syllabus, what are they really asking you for, and what do they need instead?
