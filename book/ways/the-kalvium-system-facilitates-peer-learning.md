
# The Kalvium System facilitates peer learning

"I've paid the fees, why is the mentor not teaching me?"

The question assumes learning flows one way, from the person at the front of the room. But think about how you learned many things yourself. Cramming with friends before exams. Asking the colleague next to you instead of raising it in the meeting. Watching how someone slightly better than you does it. A lot of it came from peers.

Peers reach a student in ways a mentor often cannot. A student hesitates to ask a mentor a question that might sound stupid, and asks the friend next to them without a second thought. A peer who struggled with the same concept last week remembers where the difficulty was, and explains to it, where an expert who never struggled has to guess. And thirty students who each understand something can explain it to thirty others at once, which one mentor at the front cannot.

Why we hold this as a core philosophy and not a nice-to-have: we control the environment, the mentors and the student mix. Every moment in that room is a learning opportunity, and designing for peer learning is how we squeeze the learning out of the moments no mentor is part of. It is also training for the industry itself. Tech is young and moves fast, which has made it deeply collaborative. A manager in the industry will not conduct your learning, they will broadly facilitate it.

Two conditions make it work. Helping a peer must never cost the helper. This is a reason we avoid relative grading: the moment one student's rank rises when a classmate fails, helping that classmate has a price. And it needs structure: projects, mentoring of juniors, students who cleared an interview process coaching the next batch through it, as our students did for Morgan Stanley.

Look at your classroom between sessions. Is knowledge moving sideways, or only from the front?
