
# Not up for debate

"The 9-to-6 schedule doesn't make sense. Can we reconsider?" "The no-remote policy feels dated. I think we should discuss it."

You will hear versions of these regularly, often with reasonable points attached. Before answering, sort out what is being challenged.

Processes should be questioned. How we run a sprint review, which tool tracks work, what format a reflection takes: these exist to serve our values, and when someone proposes a better process, we want to hear it. A lot of our improvements started as exactly this kind of challenge.

The values themselves are different. Showing up daily, academic and professional integrity, speaking for yourself rather than for the batch, a mentor walking in fully prepared: these define what this place is. They were chosen deliberately, and they do not change because a term's worth of people would prefer otherwise. When someone challenges one of these dressed as a process question, they are raising a values question, and that needs a different conversation.

Different does not mean shut down. "Why do we hold attendance so firmly?" deserves a real answer, and giving that answer well is part of your job. So does evidence: if how we apply a value is producing harm somewhere, showing that is not an attack on the value, and it can change how we apply it going forward. The line sits at "let's vote on whether to change this." A foundational commitment is not a matter of current preference, and treating it as one is the actual problem.

There is a cost argument too. A team has finite energy, and every hour spent re-litigating whether integrity standards should exist is an hour taken from students. Settling some things is what frees us to debate everything else properly.

So hold both positions at once. Why-questions, suggestions, and evidence about how a value lands: always open, and answered properly. Whether this is who we are: settled. Which kind of question have you been getting on your campus, and have you been answering the first kind well?
