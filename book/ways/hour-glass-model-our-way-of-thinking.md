
# The Hourglass model, our way of thinking

"The central team hasn't sent the email yet, what can I do?"

A Campus Manager says this while waiting for a green signal from the head office, passing messages between the central team and the mentors. Passing a message along carries no risk. Deciding does. So the campus waits, and the student who needed the decision waits with it.

A traditional organisation is a pyramid. The boss sits at the top and everyone looks up. Picture Kalvium differently, as an hourglass, and read it from the top:

1. The top: the students. Everything below them exists for their transformation.
2. Below them: the mentors, who hold the direct line to students.
3. The neck: the Campus Manager, the pivot point of the whole system.
4. The base: the central teams, program design, product, engineering, enablement. They hold the structure up.

Every layer serves the layer above it. Central teams have one primary customer, the Campus Manager. If the curriculum is confusing or a dashboard is clunky, the CM is expected to say so and ask for a fix, because it is being built for them. The CM in turn serves the mentors and students. A burnt-out mentor is the CM's problem to solve. And nobody in the company is more than two degrees of separation from a student.

Why build it this way? Student transformation happens on the ground, not in a document. A program architect in Bangalore cannot feel the reality of a student struggling in Phagwara. The person who feels it should hold the decision.

The neck comes with a compass, not a blank cheque. A CM adapts the calendar to a university partner's holidays without asking permission. A CM does not lower a passing grade because a partner asked. How the message is delivered can change per campus. What the message is cannot.

Wherever you sit in the hourglass, who is above you, and what did you do for them this week?
