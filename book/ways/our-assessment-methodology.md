
# Our assessment methodology

Picture the exam most of us grew up with. One sitting at the end, marks out of a hundred, a rank against the class. It answers one question: how did this student score against the others that day?

That is not the question we run on. We do not design marks-first. We assess for mastery: can the student actually do the thing this course exists to teach?

The mastery question changes what counts as evidence. Marks-first design needs one instrument, a test that produces a number. Mastery needs several, because doing the thing has several faces. Progress quizzes, to check understanding while the course is still running and a found gap can still be closed. Mentor feedback, because a mentor watching a student work sees what no paper can score, how they approach a problem, whether they can explain their own solution. And work-readiness markers, signals that map to what a workplace will ask of them, not only to what the syllabus contains.

The seed question for this page was: what is the future of MCQs? The mastery lens answers it. An MCQ checks a fact cheaply, and a student who picks the right option may still be unable to build anything with it. Use it for what it can check, never as the measure of whether someone can do the thing.

One consequence for you. A mark can be handed over without a word, and a mastery picture cannot. If assessment here tells a student where they stand, someone has to have that conversation, and that someone is usually you.

Look at how you last assessed students. Did it find out what they can do, or sort them by who did best?
